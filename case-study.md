# Draft — slide by slide

Sixteen slides. One claim on the slide, one picture or clip under it. Speak in first person. The line under **Say** is the only sentence you need to read aloud. **Show** is the only media.

Through-line, used on slide 01 and earned by slide 16: the directory was finished the day the user changed. After that, the work was taking certainty off the screen.

Ten-minute cut: 01, 03, 04, 06, 07, 10, 11, 12, 14, 16.

## Slide 01 — Title

**On the slide**
Draft. Hiring for a music video starts from a picture the label has already shipped.

**Meta**
[Your name]. Product design, product engineering. [Month – Month Year]. Atlantic Records, then 10K. Collaborators: [names].

**Design change**
The name. It began as a freelance database. It shipped as Draft: a roster you assemble, the way a team drafts a crew.

**Story**
Say the through-line once, then get out of the way. Everything after this slide is a correction of a tool that was finished for the wrong job.

**Say**
I designed the tool coordinators at 10K use to find freelancers for a video. It started as a directory for a different label, and a different kind of shoot.

**Show**
Hero, muted, 8 seconds. Headline “Draft the best talent.” Hold on the constellation long enough to read one caption. No cursor.

## Slide 02 — The first job was a clock

**On the slide**
Atlantic needed someone who could shoot tomorrow.

**Design change**
Version 1 was a list. City, day rate, a portfolio link. Filter, check, book.

**Story**
Coordinators at Atlantic were staffing behind-the-scenes, tour recaps, and social rollouts on 24 to 48 hours’ notice, on a small budget, from photographers and videographers only. The question was tactical: a lightweight directory of people who can show up without blowing the margin. That list was the right tool. A bad hire there costs a reel.

**Say**
I would have shipped that and called it done. Then 10K opened it.

**Show**
Two frames, same width. Left, labeled Atlantic: the plain list, reconstructed if you have to. Right, empty. It fills on the next slide.

## Slide 03 — The user changed

**On the slide**
10K was not booking a recap.

**Design change**
The how-might-we changed with the user. From “filter and book a shooter by tomorrow” to “share a crew for a high-budget video, chosen for a look, across a team.”

**Story**
10K runs high-concept videos. Large budgets, a treatment, a long prep. The crew is a director, a DP, wardrobe, SFX makeup, and the people around them, and they have to feel like one piece. A bad hire costs tens of thousands of dollars and the look of the record. Union status, chemistry, and a multi-week prep all showed up here. The list had no place to put them. I did not add a field for each one. Most of those facts are not knowable from a card, and pretending they are is the mistake the rest of the talk is about.

**Say**
Same instinct. A different production. The directory answered a question 10K had stopped asking.

**Show**
Fill the empty frame from slide 02. Current home, projects up, Where and Who in the bar. Corner label: 10K.

## Slide 04 — The roster was a rumor

**On the slide**
One coordinator’s network. Invisible to the other nine.

**Design change**
The object of the product stopped being search. It became a row the whole team can open. Search does nothing if the name still lives in one person’s notes.

**Story**
Ten marketing and project coordinators were losing about 100 hours a week finding people. The path was Instagram, TikTok, a tag on a venue, or a text to whoever was online. A stylist someone had already booked sat in an Apple Note, a private Google Doc, or a DM. If that person was out, the name left with them, and the next coordinator introduced 10K from scratch.

**Say**
The failure was that the roster was a person.

**Show**
One wide frame, or three tight crops: a notes app, a doc of names, a DM. Redact handles and numbers. Do not rebuild a fake Instagram.

## Slide 05 — Cold opens made one label look like ten

**On the slide**
A freelancer met a different 10K every time.

**Design change**
Outreach stopped being a blank message. The profile holds a preferred contact, and an intro that already sounds like the label, ready to copy.

**Story**
A colleague could have booked someone weeks earlier and the next message still started from zero. The questions coordinators were already texting each other became the information architecture:

1. Have we worked with them?
2. Who here referred them?
3. Does the work match this treatment?
4. Can they make this city?

Past 10K projects, Referred by, the work grid, and travel each answer one of those.

Preferred contact is sometimes two channels. Some people want Instagram and a phone. The first profile drew a single “preferred” and hid the rest. Every preferred method now sits in one row, and the intro stays tucked under it. An open message box was taller than the decision.

**Say**
If the message changes voice every time, the roster is still ten people.

**Show**
Profile, 8 seconds. Pan the work. Hold on Past 10K projects and Referred by. Open the intro, hit copy. A toast is enough.

Put the intro on the slide under the clip:

> Hi [first name]. This is the 10K team. We have a photographer need in Los Angeles, and your work looks like a fit. Are you free to talk timing and rate?

## Slide 06 — A public row can cheapen a private one

**On the slide**
These names arrived in personal DMs.

**Design change**
Draft is an internal roster. Adding someone is a team action. The signal that a relationship already exists is a 10K stamp and the question “have you worked with them,” not a public profile anyone can ping.

**Story**
This edge case almost made the directory a bad idea. A coordinator’s relationship with a director often lives in their own Instagram or their own texts. The moment that name is a row anyone can reach, a smaller ask can make the bigger relationship look uncoordinated. The creative may assume the label’s work is less serious than they were told, quote a lower rate the next time, or pull back from the person who actually has the relationship.

So the write path is conservative. Coordinators add people. The stamp marks who 10K has already worked with. Creatives adding themselves comes later, and only from a text the label sends.

**Say**
Sharing the roster and publishing the relationship are different acts. I designed for the first and refused the second.

**Show**
A card with the 10K stamp, cropped tight, beside the add dialog on “Have you worked with them previously?”

## Slide 07 — Hiring starts from a picture

**On the slide**
A creative director does not start from a city field.

**Design change**
Home leads with work the label has already shipped. The headline moved from “Who shot that?” to “Draft the best talent.” The first names the instinct. The second names the job: assembling a crew.

**Story**
I tried to draw the network before I showed the work. A world map with pins. A grid of dots connected by lines. An ink-bleed board. All of them described the product. None of them helped anyone hire. What held was a loose constellation of real frames, each with a role and a city. Captions came off, because they felt like labels on a poster, and went back on, because a beautiful frame with no role sends coordinators to the wrong Instagram.

Projects work the same way. Open a video, read the credits, click a name. The hire runs backward from proof. Hover is a silent loop of the video, not the YouTube chrome. The play button travels with the credits as you scroll. A control pinned to the top of a long credit list is a control for a page, not for the decision.

I removed the line that said credits were taken from the YouTube description. It was true, and it taught people to distrust the roster.

**Say**
If they have to leave to go find the vibe, the directory has already failed.

**Show**
Left: an early hero you killed, the map or the dot grid. Right: the constellation with one caption readable, then a cut into a project modal and one credit. 10 seconds.

## Slide 08 — A name is not a decision

**On the slide**
The go or no-go happens on the card.

**Design change**
Three densities, in order. Only the third lets someone decide without opening a tab.

1. A list. Name, role, city, contact. Fast to scan. No work in view.
2. The same facts, tighter. Still a phone book.
3. A card with a frame of the work, the role, the city, and contact on the card.

**Story**
Coordinators decide in a few seconds. Does the work fit. Are they in the city. How do I reach them. Every one of those that opens a new tab is the hour the team was losing. The map preview had already taught this: people hovered a pin to see three photos, then judged. The list had none of that, so the three frames moved onto the card.

The first version of that preview linked out to Instagram. So did the pin. The tool handed the decision to another app and lost the thread. The card, the preview, and the pin all open the profile. Instagram stays an icon, for when they are ready to write.

**Say**
I kept the facts from the list and added the only thing the list could not answer.

**Show**
Three files in a row: `list-iterations/1-first-list.png`, `list-iterations/2-compact-list.png`, `list-iterations/3-glass-cards.png`. Under the third: the preview opens the person, not their Instagram.

## Slide 09 — Genre is not a second person

**On the slide**
“Portrait photographer in Los Angeles” is one need.

**Design change**
Where and Who are one bar, two clauses. Choosing a role reveals its genres underneath it. Portrait, Concert, and Events sit under Photographer. Graphic design has no genres, so it grows no second row. One main role, at most one secondary role. One freelancer, one row.

**Story**
The first Who menu treated every genre as a sibling of the job. Concerts sat next to Photographer, so a coordinator could filter for a vibe and lose the person. Genres moved out of the dropdown and into buttons that appear once the role is chosen, so the genre reads as part of the role.

Three failures on the way there. The genre row sat outside the control and looked like a second form. Pulled inside, the label vanished the moment you clicked a genre. The secondary-role menu closed before you could reach it, because the panel thought you had left. The panel stays open across that step. Nothing is indented. “All photography” checks the genres beneath it.

“All” is a lie when there is only one option. Sweden has one city, so it gets no “All cities in Sweden.” Finland is a country, not a city, and “All cities in Finland” sits above Helsinki. The same rule shows up later on mobile moodboards: no “All” when the board has a single person in that role.

**Say**
If the filter can describe a person who does not exist, coordinators go back to guessing which link is the photographer.

**Show**
Who panel, a role open, genres under it. Beside it, the add form at the same moment: main role, genres, one secondary role. `roles-genres-outside.png` small in the corner, labeled as the version where genre looked like a separate question.

## Slide 10 — The map knew a street we had never been given

**On the slide**
A pin is a claim.

**Design change**
I built the map, then removed it from the entire product. List only. No toggle.

**Story**
The map was the most convincing screen in the project and the least honest. Pins jumped on hover. A glass card opened with three frames of work. Multi-city search zoomed out to New York and London together. It felt like a product.

What we knew was the city someone is based in. Early pins read as home addresses. I wrote a disclaimer, shortened it to one line, and took the warning icon off when it started shouting. “Map locations are approximate and indicate the city each freelancer is based in.” That sentence was an apology for the canvas. Once it sounded like an apology, the map had to go. A list of cities does not pretend to know a block.

The preview stayed. Three frames of work, on the card, is the part that was doing the job.

**Say**
I would rather lose a screen people liked than keep a screen that over-claims.

**Show**
A strip of `map-stages/1-full.jpg` through `4-full.jpg`, small. Then the current results grid, large. Caption: the pictures stayed. The geography did not.

## Slide 11 — I built a rate, then took it off

**On the slide**
A number on the card is a promise.

**Design change**
I designed a full rate control and then deleted it. Nothing on the card invents a price.

**Story**
Coordinators asked for budget in the first week, and I gave them a precise one. Dollar tiers. A slider for hourly. A flat fee with anchors at $1,000, $5,000, and $10,000, so a tier would mean something. Radios, then a dropdown, then the tier set beside the role with a bullet so it would scan like the other facts.

Most of the time the coordinator does not have the number yet. The deal is a conversation. A blank tier looks like free, or like unknown, and both are lies. A filled tier, typed by someone guessing, becomes the number the next coordinator quotes. I removed the control, the slider, and the symbols. When someone actually knows the deal, it lives as a compensation note on the profile. The card stays quiet.

Same failure as the map, one level down. The interface was more confident than the data.

**Say**
Unknown has to look unknown. The moment it looks like zero, the roster has started negotiating.

**Show**
The slider and the three anchors, if you still have the mock, labeled “tried, did not ship.” If you do not, this slide is type only. Do not fake a rate onto a current card.

## Slide 12 — The form was a biography nobody had

**On the slide**
Coordinators arrive with a link and a memory.

**Design change**
Add a creative collapsed from a full profile to two fields. An Instagram link, which checks itself when the link is real. And one question: have you worked with them before.

**Story**
The long form was a complete person. City, travel, bio, main role, genres, a second role, three contact methods, a rate. It scrolled, and the secondary role scrolled out of sight, which was the interface telling on itself. Filling it meant the coordinator already knew everything the profile was supposed to teach the rest of the team. They had a DM.

Willing to travel came off the form for the same reason. Home city and “also goes to” are different facts, and people were collapsing them because the form offered one mood of certainty. Travel still shows on the profile when we actually know it. The form no longer demands it at the door.

The check on the Instagram field is the validation that matches how names arrive: pasted, half-typed, sometimes not a URL. A bad link writes a row the whole team will search.

**Say**
A roster only becomes shared if the write is easy enough to happen. The long form protected the data by making sure nobody entered it.

**Show**
The short dialog, filled. The check appearing at the right of a valid Instagram link. Cut to the card that shows up after submit. Optional corner: the Supabase table with the new row and the 10K column. Caption: a new creative is a new row.

## Slide 13 — A star in a private note is the silo again

**On the slide**
The short list has to be a thing the next coordinator can open.

**Design change**
Star on the card or the profile. Drop them on a board named for the shoot. The board keeps the work attached. Role tabs sit across the top: Photographers, Videographers, Graphic designers.

**Story**
Starring someone into a personal list recreates slide 04. The board is the unit of hiring. One shoot, a handful of people, shared. A mixed column of names is unscannable the moment the crew is more than one craft, so the tabs split the same list by role. On a phone those tabs are underlines. Buttons read as a second toolbar.

Unsaving someone threw a confirmation into the top-left corner, far from the star that triggered it. I removed it. A destructive confirm that appears where the eye is not looking gets accepted by accident, or ignored. The star is the control and the confirmation.

**Say**
Hand the next coordinator a list, not a rumor.

**Show**
8 seconds. Star a person. Add them to a board named for a real shoot. Cut to the board with the work still on the cards, one role tab selected.

## Slide 14 — A board of faces is still not a treatment

**On the slide**
You cannot tell if a crew belongs together from a stack of profiles.

**Design change**
Generate moodboard. Pick the lineup on the board, with a face and a full name on each checkbox. The board becomes one surface of their work. Shuffle the layout. Change the lineup without leaving the picture.

**Story**
The first generator asked you to scroll the board and select people, and the people disappeared as soon as the moodboard opened. Selection moved inside the generation step. Small choices first, then checkboxes, so “check all” is possible and a long name is readable. “All” hides when there is only one person. The generate button does not survive into the moodboard. You are done choosing.

On the phone the crew pills came off. They repeated the lineup in type and crushed the pictures. Share is a sheet. Copy the link, Messages, or Gmail. The preview is a collage of the work, on black, with a thin rule between the pictures and the words. The line on the text is “Let’s draft a board.”

I prototyped the message as a real thread to a contact named Anna. A “link copied” toast never shows whether the board still looks like a board once it leaves the tool. The collage in the bubble is the test.

**Say**
The board answers who we are considering. The moodboard answers whether they look like one video.

**Show**
Desktop: check two roles, generate, hold on the collage. Then the phone sheet: Messages, the black collage, the thread to Anna. 10 seconds. Split them if the cut feels crowded.

## Slide 15 — Mobile is a different sentence

**On the slide**
A dropdown squeezed onto a phone is still a desktop dropdown.

**Design change**
Search on the phone is a sheet. Where, then Who, as two steps. The field fills the screen, the caret becomes a back arrow, and a scrim covers the logo and the hero. Search and Clear appear only once both clauses are chosen. Clear All opens Where again, so clearing returns you to the start of the sentence.

**Story**
Browsing every creative and searching from home were writing into the same field. Filtering on the results page rewrote the query above it, and a coordinator who had come in from home lost the search they had just typed. Browse filters stay on the browse. The home query stays what they typed.

I built a slide between Creatives, Search, and Boards, ordered like the floating menu. It slid too far and meant nothing, so the motion came out. The menu stays put. Search rises from the bottom, without the large circle that made it look like a spotlight. Movement stays only when it explains where the surface came from.

The hamburger opened underneath the menu it was supposed to reveal, because the menu was positioned against the screen and the header sat below the status bar. The menu now hangs off the header. Overlays, including the share sheet, sit above the floating menu.

Results are one person per row. Two per row showed more of the roster and killed the three frames. The pictures are the decision. The density lost.

**Say**
I stopped scaling the desktop down and wrote the phone search as its own sentence.

**Show**
Phone frame. Tap Search. The sheet rises. Set a city, set a role, and only then does Search appear. One result card, work visible. 8 seconds.

## Slide 16 — What I would keep

**On the slide**
Show the work. Write the row once. Claim only what you know.

**Design change**
Two decisions, then the next ship.

**Story**
Lead with the work, and keep the filters. A moodboard with no metadata cannot answer who is in this city. A table with no pictures cannot answer whether this looks like the treatment. Projects open the home page. Where and Who run on the same people. The map and the rate came off because they answered in a precision we did not have.

One roster, written once. A creative added by one coordinator becomes a row the whole team can search. The 10K stamp, Referred by, past projects, and the board are how a personal find becomes institutional. The write is short because the long form was where the roster went to die.

Next: a creative gets a text from 10K and enters the same facts on their phone. That submission writes the same kind of row. The roster can grow when nobody has time to type, and it still begins with the label.

**Say**
The hard part was not the directory. A list of freelancers is easy. The hard part was an interface that would not know more than the team knew, and would still get a name out of one person’s DMs and into the next person’s hands.

**Show**
Three short loops, numbered, no other labels.

1. Open a project, click a credit.
2. Read the profile, copy the intro.
3. The board, then the text on a phone.

End on the survey in the phone frame. The iMessage from 10K, then the form. Caption: the creative writes the row.
